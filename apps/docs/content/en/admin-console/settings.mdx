# Settings in the console

Most of what makes one tenant behave differently from another is a setting: how long a sign-in lasts, whether people may register themselves, what a password must contain, how many wrong guesses lock an account. They all live on one page, in sections you can reach from a list of names that stays in view as you scroll, and each section saves on its own. It needs the `manage-tenant` capability; the menu entry is **Settings** under **TENANT**.

## 1. Find the section

The list of sections is made of links, so a section can be opened from a bookmark or sent to somebody. It stays at the top of the screen as a strip, and beside the sections as a rail when the page is wide. It names the sections under five headings:

- **Tenant**: **General**, **Terms** and **Profile**.
- **Sign-in**: **Usernames**, **Registration and recovery**, **Authenticators**, **Passkey requirement**, **Password policy** and **Brute force**.
- **Sessions and tokens**: **Sessions**, **Remember me** and **Token lifetimes**.
- **Applications**: **Client registration** and **Agents**.
- **Records**: **Audit retention**.
- **Assistant**: **Console assistant**, listed last and only where the assistant is on.

Choosing a name moves to that section and puts the focus on its heading. The section in view is marked, and a section with changes you have not saved has a dot, so one scrolled out of sight is not forgotten.

<ConsoleShot
  name="settings-rail"
  alt="The Settings page on a wide screen: a rail of section links beside the sections, grouped under Tenant, Sign-in, Sessions and tokens, Applications, Records and Assistant, with the section in view marked."
  caption="On a wide screen the list stays beside the sections, grouped, with the section in view marked."
/>

Each field says what it does and the range the server accepts, as one line such as _Allowed: 1 minute to 30 days_. A longer explanation is folded under **More**. A duration is typed as a number with a unit menu beside it (`s`, `min`, `h` or `d`) and the console opens it in the largest unit that holds the value exactly, so 1800 seconds shows as `30 min`. A unit the value is not a whole count of is greyed out in the menu, so choosing a unit never changes the setting; what is saved is always seconds. A value outside the range is marked before you save.

## 2. Change a section and save it

Change the fields you want, then choose the **Save** button of that section. Nothing else on the page is sent. If somebody else changed the same section while you had it open, the console says so and shows what they set, so you do not overwrite it unseen.

Some common changes:

- To let people create their own accounts, turn on **Self-registration** under **Registration and recovery**, and turn on **Verify email addresses** if you want a link mailed to them. Mail needs an [Email relay](./email.mdx). The sign-in page then shows a link reading _Create an account_, and one reading _Forgot password?_ when password reset is on too. An app can send a person straight to the registration page with `prompt=create`; see [a sign-up button](../guides/sign-a-user-in.md#a-sign-up-button). A form that is refused comes back with the problem beside the field. A taken username is always named. With **Verify email addresses** on, the account is created when the person opens the link and confirms, and an address that already has an account is not named: the page says _Check your email_ as it does for a new address, and that address's owner gets a mail saying someone tried to register it. With it off, nothing can be mailed, so the form says the address is registered. Verification and registration links ask for one click, so mail scanners that open links cannot spend them. The page names the address and the tenant above the button. A verification link that was used or has expired shows _Send a new link_, which mails another to the address the old one was for. At most three an hour are mailed to one address, and the page answers the same whether or not one was, so it does not say which addresses are waiting to be verified. A mailed verification or reset link shows _Continue to sign in_ only in the browser where the person started, and only while that sign-in is open. Anywhere else the page says to return to the application and sign in again.
- To change what a password must satisfy, edit **Minimum length** and the other fields under **Password policy**; [Choose a password policy](#4-choose-a-password-policy) says what to pick. A rule applies to the next password somebody sets, never to one already chosen.
- To change what a new authenticator app, passkey or recovery set is made with, edit the fields under **Authenticators**; [Authenticators](#authenticators) says what each does. A change applies to the next enrolment, never to one that exists.
- To shorten how long a person stays signed in, lower **Idle timeout** and **Maximum lifetime** under **Sessions**.
- To let applications register themselves with a token, set **Policy** under **Client registration**; the [Registration tokens](./registration-tokens.mdx) page does the rest.
- To keep the audit trail for longer or shorter, change **Keep events for** under **Audit retention**, and choose which kinds of event are recorded. Changes and reads by administrators are always recorded.

## 3. Unlock everyone

Under **Brute force**, **Failures before a lockout** and the lockout lengths decide when an account locks. If a mistake locked many people at once, **Clear all lockouts** forgets every failed sign-in on record in the tenant and asks you to confirm. One account's lockout is cleared on that person's **Credentials** tab instead.

## 4. Choose a password policy

A long password protects better than a complicated one, and the console's defaults follow the NIST guidance on that (SP 800-63B-4). New tenants start with a minimum of 15 characters.

- Length: a password used alone should have at least 15 characters. Where every sign-in also needs a second factor, 8 is enough. If your minimum is lower than 15 and a second factor is not required of everyone, a note under the section says so. The note is advice, and you can still save. To require the second factor, turn on **Ask every subject for a one-time password** in [Sign-in flow](./sign-in-flow.mdx).
- Character rules: the digit, uppercase, lowercase and special-character rules are off by default. NIST advises against them, so turn one on only if you must. When one is on, the password pages list it for the person.
- Expiry: **Password lifetime** is 0 by default, so a password never expires. NIST advises against changing passwords on a schedule.
- Common passwords: a password is always refused when it is on a list of about 38,000 common ones, or when it is only the tenant's name, the username, or the name part of the email. Only the whole password is compared, ignoring case: `baseball` is refused and `baseball and a long walk home` is not. This has no setting.
- Known breaches: **Check against known breaches** refuses a password that appears in a published list of passwords taken from breaches, once it has passed the rules above. [Passwords known from breaches](#passwords-known-from-breaches) says what the three values do. It is on, against the loaded dataset, for every tenant, and it refuses nothing until an operator loads a dataset.
- Typing: passwords are stored in Unicode NFC form, so `é` typed as one character or as `e` and an accent is one password. A password set before this change still signs in.

A person with a passkey signs in without a password, so none of this applies to that sign-in.

## Every setting

Each line gives the label, then the name the admin API and `odudu seed tenant --set` use. A tenant's current values are in [the settings transcript](../reference/transcripts/admin-paths/get-settings-and-patch-settings.md).

### General

- **Display name** (`display_name`): what the tenant's own pages call it. Empty uses the tenant name.
- **Enabled** (`enabled`): off disables the whole tenant.

### Sessions

- **Idle timeout** (`sso_session_idle_seconds`): a session ends after this long without activity. At most the maximum.
- **Maximum lifetime** (`sso_session_max_seconds`): a session ends this long after sign-in, however active. Both run from 1 minute to 30 days.
- **Sessions per browser** (`max_sessions_per_browser`): how many people one browser may be signed in as at once, 1 to 32.
- **Time to finish a sign-in** (`login_ttl_seconds`): an unfinished sign-in must be started again after this, 1 minute to 24 hours.
- **Recent sign-in window** (`session_freshness_seconds`): how recent a person's sign-in must be before the "me" API accepts a new email address, a new username, ending all other sessions, revoking a consent or removing a credential. 300 seconds by default, 30 seconds to 1 hour.

### Remember me

- **Offer remember me** (`remember_me_allowed`): the sign-in page shows the choice only while this is on. A person who ticks it stays signed in for the longer times below.
- **Idle timeout** (`remember_me_idle_seconds`) and **Maximum lifetime** (`remember_me_max_seconds`): the same two limits for a remembered session, up to a year.

### Registration and recovery

- **Self-registration** (`registration_allowed`): anybody may create an account on the sign-in page.
- **Verify email addresses** (`verify_email`): the account is created when the person confirms a mailed link. While it is on, a password reset link is mailed only to an address the person has verified; see **Password reset by mail** below.
- **Verification link lifetime** (`verify_email_ttl_seconds`): how long that link works, up to 7 days.
- **Username at registration** (`registration_username`): what the registration form asks for. **Required**, the default, asks for a username. **Optional** shows the field marked optional, and a blank one gets a handle Odudu generates. **Generated** shows no username field and gives every new account a handle, `u-` and ten letters and digits, whatever the form sends. Optional and Generated need both **Sign in with a verified email address** and **Verify email addresses** on, and keep them on, because a person with no chosen username signs in by email; the server refuses the change otherwise and says which setting is off. A person who chose no username has the handle as their username, and an application receives it as `preferred_username` unless the person sets their own; an application must not treat it as an identifier, which is `sub`. A handle can be changed only where **Usernames can be renamed** is on. If an administrator changes such a person's email address, the new address is mailed a link and replaces the old one only when the person follows it, so until then they sign in with their username, which the **Username** field on their record shows.
- **Password reset by mail** (`reset_password_allowed`): a person who forgot their password may ask for a reset link. With **Verify email addresses** on, the link goes only to a verified address. A request for any other is answered the same way and sends nothing, so nobody can tell from the answer whether an address is held or verified. That person asks for a new verification link, or you send one or a reset from their **Credentials** tab. With it off, the tenant accepts unverified addresses and a reset link goes to any address on an account. When a reset sets the password, the person is told by mail at the address on the account, verified or not; the notice carries no link, and **Message overrides** can reword it.
- **Reset link lifetime** (`reset_password_ttl_seconds`): how long a reset link works, up to 24 hours.

When somebody sets a new password from a mailed link, whether a reset link or an administrator's link that asks for one, every session they hold ends and every token grant they gave is revoked, offline ones too. Anyone else signed in to that account is signed out.

A person who changes their password on the change-password page is offered "End my other sessions", checked. Leave it checked and their other sessions end, the one they are using stays, and so do grants that no session holds; clear it and nothing ends. No setting turns the box off.

If the account registered itself and its address was never verified, the link also removes any authenticator app, passkey and recovery codes and marks the address verified. The page says so. Whoever registered the address first may have added them. In a tenant that does not verify addresses, that is every self-registered account until its first reset. Accounts an administrator created keep their factors.

The link stops working once it is used, and it asks for one click first, so a mail scanner that opens it cannot spend it.

### Terms

<ConsoleShot
  name="settings-profile"
  alt="The Terms and Profile sections of the Settings page. Terms has an address field and a short version field. Profile lists the twenty fields a person can edit under Name, On the web, About the person, Contact and Address, in columns."
  caption="The Terms section, and the Profile section with its fields grouped."
/>

- **Terms address** (`terms_uri`): where the terms are published. An https address, or http to this machine, with no fragment. Leave it empty for none; the admin API clears it with an empty string.
- **Terms version** (`terms_version`): up to 64 characters, `1` by default. A person accepts one version.

Setting the address asks nobody. An administrator ticks **Accept the terms** on a person's record under **Required actions**, and that person's next sign-in shows a page that links the terms, names the version and has one box, _I accept these terms_. Ticking it records the version the page showed, with when, and clears the action. The form carries the version it showed: if the version changed while the page was open, the page comes back at the new version saying so and records nothing, so nobody accepts terms they did not see. a person with no box ticked cannot go on, and the page has no way to leave it. Changing the version adds the action to nobody, so tick it again for the people who should accept the new terms. A person who has accepted the version in force is not asked even when the action is owed, and one owed an action while the tenant has no address is not asked either; a sign-in clears the action instead of stopping. An application cannot start **Accept the terms**: `/authorize` refuses `action=accept-terms`, because the acceptance is the tenant's own and an application could otherwise record consent on its behalf.

### Profile

- **Required profile fields** (`required_profile_fields`): the claims the **Complete the profile** action needs a person to have before it ends, chosen from the twenty a person can edit through the me API, listed under five headings and laid out in columns: **Name** (**Full name**, **Given name**, **Family name**, **Middle name**, **Nickname** and **Preferred username**), **On the web** (**Profile page**, **Picture address** and **Website**), **About the person** (**Gender**, **Birthdate**, **Time zone** and **Language**), **Contact** (**Phone number**) and **Address** (**Address**, **Street**, **City**, **Region**, **Postal code** and **Country**). **Given name** and **Family name** by default. The email address is never among them: an address is changed through its own proof.

The page the person sees lists every field they can edit, with what they hold, and marks the required ones. It ends when every required field has a value after the form is sent; the other fields are optional, and every value sent is checked by the rules `PATCH /me/profile` applies, all the refusals at once. Nothing is written until the whole form is acceptable. Choose none and the page is only reviewed and submitted. An empty list is saved as an empty list; the admin API takes `[]`.

### Authenticators

<ConsoleShot
  name="settings-authenticators"
  alt="The Authenticators and Passkey requirement sections of the Settings page. Authenticators has three groups: Authenticator app with algorithm, code length and code period, Passkeys, and Recovery codes. Passkey requirement is a single switch."
  caption="Authenticators in three groups, and the passkey requirement in a section of its own."
/>

The section has three groups, one for each kind of authenticator.

- **Authenticator app**: **Algorithm** (`totp_algorithm`), **SHA1**, **SHA256** or **SHA512**, where `SHA1` is the default and the one every authenticator app supports. **Code length** (`totp_digits`), **6 digits** or **8 digits**. **Code period** (`totp_period`), **30 seconds** or **60 seconds**.
- **Passkeys**: **User verification** (`webauthn_user_verification`), **Required**, **Preferred** or **Discouraged**, the WebAuthn values of the same names (`required`, `preferred`, `discouraged`), with Required the default. **Stored on the device** (`webauthn_resident_key`), the same three values and the same default.
- **Recovery codes**: **Codes in a set** (`recovery_code_count`), how many codes a newly issued set holds, 8 to 16 and 10 by default. **Warn when fewer than** (`recovery_code_warn_below`), where a person left with fewer unspent recovery codes than this is flagged by `recovery_codes_low` in the "me" API's credentials answer. 3 by default, 0 to 10 and no more than the size of a set, and 0 turns the warning off.

### Passkey requirement

- **Require a passkey** (`require_passkey`): off by default. On, a person who signs in and holds no passkey for the address they signed in at is asked to register one before the sign-in completes. It does nothing where the deployment has no public base URL (`ODUDU_PUBLIC_BASE_URL`), or one that is an IP address, unless the tenant has a domain of its own: nobody is asked, whatever the switch says. [Asking everyone for a passkey](#asking-everyone-for-a-passkey) says what that does and does not touch.

The enrolment settings above apply to the next enrolment; **Require a passkey**, in its own section, is a sign-in policy and acts on people who are already enrolled, at their next sign-in. An authenticator app keeps the algorithm, digits and period it was made with and is checked by them, so changing the tenant's choice strands nobody; a person who wants the new parameters enrols the app again. A passkey keeps the user verification and resident key it was made with, and a set of recovery codes keeps its size. A step of the [sign-in flow](./sign-in-flow.mdx#authenticator-settings-on-a-step) can override any of the enrolment settings above for itself, and what it leaves out takes the value set here. A person who is enrolling an app is shown what it must support when the parameters are not `SHA1`, 6 digits and 30 seconds, because many apps do not support the others and a code the app cannot make never confirms. Choose them only for an app you know.

A passkey made without user verification can still give a verified sign-in, but when an assertion lacks verification it proves possession of a key and nothing else, so that sign-in is one factor and not two. The person is still asked for an authenticator app code where they have one or the tenant requires one. A sign-in by that passkey alone reports `acr` `1` and no `user` method; with the authenticator app step also completed, the token reports `acr` `2`. Choose `preferred` or `discouraged` only if you accept that. Sign-in always asks the browser for `preferred` verification, and each passkey is then judged by the policy it was made under: a passkey made while verification was required is refused when its assertion lacks verification, whatever the tenant has chosen since, and a passkey made under `preferred` still signs in after the tenant moves to `required`.

Passkey sign-in names no username, so only a passkey stored on the device can answer it. Odudu asks the browser to report whether a new passkey is stored on the device and refuses one reported as not, under every value of this setting, with a message asking the person to add one that can be stored. The value is what the authenticator is asked for. A browser that does not report is trusted, so a passkey that is not stored can still get through there and then cannot sign in.

#### Asking everyone for a passkey

With **Require a passkey** on, a sign-in that completes adds the `configure-passkey` required action for a person who holds no passkey under the address they signed in at, and the next page is the passkey registration page. Nothing else changes:

- A session that is already open is not touched, and no token is revoked. The person is asked at their next sign-in, not before; a client that sends a person to `/authorize` with a session still open sees no extra step.
- The action is added once, however often the person signs in, and a person who already holds a passkey is never asked. A person who removes their last passkey is asked again at their next sign-in. A passkey registered at another address, for example before the tenant moved to a domain of its own, is hidden at sign-in and so does not count.
- The page has no way to skip it. This is a mandate of the tenant, so a person whose browser or device cannot make a passkey cannot finish a sign-in. An administrator can clear the action on their record under **Required actions**, which lets the sign-in under way finish; the next sign-in asks again while the switch is on, so for such a person the way through is to turn the switch off and clear the owed action, or to clear it at each of their sign-ins while the switch stays on.
- Turning the switch off does not remove an action already owed. A person who was asked and has not registered still meets the page at their next sign-in, until they register or an administrator clears it.
- A deployment that cannot show the page never adds the action, so the switch cannot lock everybody out. That is a deployment with no public base URL, or one whose base URL is an IP address and a tenant with no domain of its own, because the relying party is derived from a host name. The console does not know this, so it lets you turn the switch on there; it then does nothing.
- An application can still send a person to register a passkey with `action=configure-passkey`. Where the tenant already owes the person one, the page is the tenant's and has no way back.
- Service accounts and agent tokens never sign in at a page and are never asked.

### Usernames

- **Usernames can be renamed** (`username_editable`): an administrator may change a username.
- **Sign in with a verified email address** (`login_with_email`): the sign-in form takes a verified address in place of a username.

### Password policy

<ConsoleShot
  name="settings-password"
  alt="The Password policy section of the Settings page, in four groups: Length and complexity, Must not contain, Reuse and age, and Breached passwords, where the setting for a failed remote lookup is dimmed because the check is not set to the remote lookup."
  caption="The password policy in groups; the remote lookup setting waits for the remote lookup."
/>

The fields sit in four groups: **Length and complexity**, **Must not contain**, **Reuse and age** and **Breached passwords**.

- **Minimum length** (`password_min_length`), 8 to 256 characters.
- **Require a digit**, **Require an uppercase letter**, **Require a lowercase letter** and **Require a special character** (`password_require_digit`, `password_require_uppercase`, `password_require_lowercase`, `password_require_special`).
- **Refuse the username** (`password_not_username`) and **Refuse the email address** (`password_not_email`): a password may not be only that.
- **Passwords remembered** (`password_history_depth`): a new password may not repeat one of this many before it. 0 remembers none, 24 is the most.
- **Password lifetime** (`password_max_age_days`): a password older than this must be changed. 0 never expires one.
- **Check against known breaches** (`breached_check`): **Off**, **Dataset loaded on the server** (`dataset`, the default) or **Remote range lookup** (`remote`). [Passwords known from breaches](#passwords-known-from-breaches) says what each does.
- **When a remote lookup fails** (`breached_on_error`): **Accept the password** (`open`, the default) or **Refuse it and ask to try again** (`closed`). It applies to the remote lookup only, so the console dims it, and says so, unless **Check against known breaches** is set to the remote lookup.
- `otp_required` is the **Ask every subject for a one-time password** switch of [Sign-in flow](./sign-in-flow.mdx).

#### Passwords known from breaches

A password that passed the other rules is also refused when it is a password known from breaches. The refusal comes after the length, character, username, email and common-password rules and before the reuse check, on the same four pages that apply those rules: registration, a reset link, an administrator's actions link and the change-password page. The page says "That password has appeared in a data breach. Choose a different one." and never says which source said so. Administrators never type a password for anybody, so there is no fifth place; `odudu seed` checks the policy rules only.

- **Dataset loaded on the server** asks a table in the server's own database for the password's SHA-1 hash, split as the public range lists split it. Nothing leaves the server. The table is empty until an operator runs `odudu breached-passwords load` with a file of hashes ([Deploying](../operating/deploying.md#load-a-breached-password-dataset)); an empty table refuses nothing, and `odudu breached-passwords status` says whether it holds any. One table serves every tenant.
- **Remote range lookup** asks a range service at one fixed host, `api.pwnedpasswords.com`, for the first five characters of the hash. The service answers with every suffix that starts with them and Odudu looks for its own in the answer, so neither the password nor its full hash leaves the server. The request carries no cookie, no account and no tenant name. It waits at most 3 seconds, reads at most 128 KiB of answer, and holds no database connection while it waits. A tenant has at most 4 lookups in flight and the whole server at most 8, so a form submitted in a burst fails the lookup rather than queueing: a lookup refused by either limit is a failed lookup, settled by **When a remote lookup fails** like a timeout, and shows as `error_open` or `error_closed` in the trail. An address that resolves to a private or loopback address is refused, as for a client's key set. It is off for every tenant until an administrator chooses it, because it is the one place a sign-in page's traffic causes a request to another service.
- **Off** skips the check. The audit row of the password write says `off`, so a tenant that stopped checking can be seen.

When the remote lookup fails, **When a remote lookup fails** decides. **Accept the password** lets it through and the audit row of the write says `error_open`. **Refuse it and ask to try again** refuses it with "The password could not be checked against known breaches. Try again in a moment." The dataset has no such failure.

Each password write records what the check decided in `detail.breached_check` of its audit row: `passed`, `dataset_empty`, `error_open` or `off`. `dataset_empty` is the dataset source finding nothing because the dataset holds no hash at all, so a tenant that has never had a dataset loaded is not mistaken for one that checked. The password is never recorded, and neither is any part of its hash. A refused password writes no row, as a refused rule does not, so `breached` and `error_closed`, the two other values the check can report, are never stored. A lookup that failed on a saturated limit is `error_open` on an accepted write, like any other failure.

### Brute force

- **Failures before a lockout** (`brute_force_max_failures`), 1 to 100.
- **First lockout** (`brute_force_lockout_seconds`): how long the first lockout lasts, up to a day.
- **Longest lockout** (`brute_force_max_lockout_seconds`): each repeat lasts longer, up to this.
- **Failures forgotten after** (`brute_force_failure_reset_seconds`): failures this old stop counting, 1 minute to 30 days.

### Token lifetimes

- **Access token** (`access_token_ttl_seconds`), **ID token** (`id_token_ttl_seconds`): up to an hour each.
- **Refresh token** (`refresh_token_ttl_seconds`): how long a refresh token lives.
- **Authorization code** (`authorization_code_ttl_seconds`): a client has this long to trade a code for tokens, up to 10 minutes.

A client can carry lifetimes of its own, which win over these.

### Client registration

- **Policy** (`client_registration_policy`): **Closed: only an administrator creates clients**, **With a registration token**, **Open: any application may register** or **MCP clients: public clients of the MCP server may register**. The last admits only a public client with `https` or loopback `http` redirects, which may ask for `mcp:read` and whose only audience is the tenant's MCP address; see [consent and audit](../concepts/consent-and-audit.mdx).
- **Accept client metadata documents** (`cimd_enabled`): a client may use an HTTPS address as its `client_id`, and the document served there describes it. It works under any policy, and a client that comes this way gets the MCP audience only while the policy admits MCP clients. Off, such an address is answered as an unknown client. It saves with the policy, in the same section.
- **Most clients** (`max_clients`): the most clients an administrator or a registration token can make, the built-in ones included. 0 allows none, and a new tenant starts at 200. Clients that arrive on their own are not counted, so they cannot keep an administrator from making one. Each kind has a limit of its own: 1,000 that registered themselves, and 1,000 from a metadata document. At its limit a new one takes the place of the oldest that nobody approved, signed in through or granted anything, and is refused when there is none.

#### A client named by an address

A tenant can let an app identify itself with a web address instead of registering first. The `cimd_enabled` setting, off by default, turns it on. A `client_id` that is an https address is then fetched: the document served there, a Client ID Metadata Document, gives the app's name and redirect addresses. Odudu keeps the result as a public client and sends people back only to a listed address. Anyone can claim a name, so the consent screen also shows the host the document came from and every redirect address. A client nobody has used for thirty days is removed and returns when its address is next used. With the setting off, sign-in and token requests for such an address are refused like any unknown client; tokens already issued stay valid until they expire.

#### A client that registers itself for MCP

A tenant can also let an agent register itself with `client_registration_policy` set to `mcp`. The registration endpoint then admits only a public client, with https or loopback http redirect addresses, which may hold `mcp:read` and never `mcp:write` (a request that also names `mcp:write` is registered without it, and the response's `scope` says what was kept), and whose only audience is the tenant's MCP address. Each address may register ten clients an hour and the tenant sixty, and the tenant may hold 1,000 clients that registered themselves, whatever `max_clients` says. Any public client that registered itself, under `open` as well as `mcp`, and is never approved, signed in through or granted anything is deleted by `odudu reap` after a week; one with an approval or a grant that has not yet been reaped stays. A tenant imported from an export brings such a client back without its approval or consent, so it is deleted a week after the import unless it is used, and an import whose document holds more than 1,000 of them is refused. Such a client, and a metadata document client on a tenant with this policy, is given that audience and `mcp:read` by the policy and not by anything it sends. If the policy is later changed, such a client stops being offered that audience and `mcp:read` at its next sign-in and its next refresh, so a refresh chain started under the policy carries neither once the policy is off; disable or delete the client to end the chain at once. A metadata document client that signs in with a key rather than as a public client is not given them. A client of these kinds is never offered `mcp:write`. `mcp:read` is optional, so the consent screen offers it as a choice.

An administrator who changes the policy or the switch leaves a `tenant.amend_settings` audit event whose detail holds the value each had before and after. The settings that decide who may become a client, how fresh a sign-in must be, how a new authenticator or password is made and checked, the terms, the wording people read and the agent limits are recorded the same way. A setting that is not among them is not named in that detail.

### Agents

The limits on agent instances and on what an agent may write under a person's grant. Each is held to the range shown by a CHECK in the database, so no writer can step outside it.

- **Delegation depth** (`agent_max_delegation_depth`): how many instances deep one delegation may go, 1 to 8 and 3 by default. 8 is the structural limit of the `act` claim.
- **Children per instance** (`agent_max_children`): how many live child instances one instance may have, 1 to 200 and 20 by default.
- **Live instances** (`agent_max_live_instances`): how many instances the tenant may hold at once, 1 to 100,000 and 1,000 by default. Mints that run at the same moment under different parents are not serialised, so the count can briefly pass the limit by up to the number of mints in flight.
- **Instance lifetime** (`agent_instance_ttl_seconds`) and **Instance idle timeout** (`agent_instance_idle_seconds`): how long an instance lives and how long it may go without a call, 1 minute to 24 hours, 15 minutes and 30 minutes by default. Neither outlasts the owner's session.
- **Approval lifetime** (`agent_approval_ttl_seconds`): how long a person has to approve one action an agent asked to take, 1 to 15 minutes and 10 minutes by default.
- **Writes per grant** (`agent_writes_per_grant`) and **Creates per grant** (`agent_creates_per_grant`): how many writes an agent may make under one grant, creates included, and how many of those may create something, 1 to 1,000, and 20 and 10 by default.
- **Writes per day** (`agent_window_writes`): how many writes one agent type may make for one person in a 24-hour UTC window, across their sign-ins, 1 to 100,000 and 1,000 by default.

### Audit retention

- **Keep events for** (`audit_retention_days`): 1 to 3650 days.
- **Events recorded** (`audit_event_types`): **Changes by administrators** and **Reads by administrators** are always recorded. You choose **Sign-ins and refused sign-ins**, **Sessions starting and ending**, **Tokens issued and revoked**, and **Passwords, keys and codes set or changed**.

The last section of the page, and only where the server has the assistant switched on: an operator who configured no model leaves it out, and nobody sees a section or a link for it. It needs `manage-assistant` to change. A system administrator who holds `manage-tenants` and `manage-assistant` can read and change another tenant's assistant settings through the admin API, as for any other tenant setting; the conversations themselves belong to the administrator who started them. A person with `read-assistant-activity` and not `manage-assistant` sees every value as text under a note that names the capability, and a person with neither sees no section and the console asks nothing of the server for it. The settings are a tenant's own, and each starts at a default a tenant that has never opened this page already runs on. The section names the default beside each setting, and a save sends all ten settings at once, on the ETag it was read with, so another administrator's change is shown beside yours and is not overwritten.

**Availability**

- **Offer the assistant** (`assistant_enabled`): on by default. Off, the status route answers `{ "enabled": false }` for this tenant alone, and every other tenant of the deployment is unchanged. Starting a conversation and sending a message are then refused with the answer of a path that was never served, in this tenant only. What is already recorded stays, and a person can still read a conversation and stop a running turn.
- **Starts in** (`assistant_default_mode`): **Ask: the assistant only reads** or **Act: it may propose changes, each waiting for approval**. Ask is the default. A person can switch the mode in the panel.

**Privacy and records**

- **Mask email addresses** (`assistant_mask_emails`): off by default. On, an email address in what the assistant reads, and in the page text it is given, is replaced before it is sent to the model provider by its first letter, three asterisks and its domain, so the domain still shows. The setting is read for each turn, so a change applies to the next message. Addresses a person types themselves, and messages from before the change, are not rewritten.
- **Auditors can read conversations** (`assistant_auditor_visible`): on by default. People who hold `read-assistant-activity` see what was asked and what the assistant did, and everyone who uses the assistant is told so.
- **Keep traces for** (`assistant_trace_retention_days`): 1 to 365 days and 30 by default. A trace is the step-by-step record of a conversation. The audit trail keeps its own rows for as long as its retention says.

**Limits**

- **Fast model, each month** (`assistant_monthly_tokens_fast`), **Standard model, each month** (`assistant_monthly_tokens_standard`) and **Deep model, each month** (`assistant_monthly_tokens_deep`): the tokens the tenant may spend each month on each model slot, 0 to 1,000,000,000. They start at 2,000,000, 5,000,000 and 1,000,000. The assistant stops when a month's allowance for a model is used: the answer ends with a message that names the limit and says who can change it, and the assistant never falls back to a cheaper model on its own. Each model call sets aside what it could cost, up to about 132,000 tokens, until it finishes and its real cost replaces that, so an allowance below about 150,000 tokens refuses every answer and one below about 530,000 can refuse some. A month is a calendar month in UTC. The panel warns when a limit passes 80 percent. Two limits sit beside these and have no setting: one administrator may use a quarter of a month's allowance for a model in one UTC day, and one answer may use 1,000,000 tokens and one conversation 2,000,000 in a month. [Operating the assistant's budget](../operating/assistant-budget.md) says how a call that was cut off is counted.
- **Answers at once** (`assistant_max_concurrent`): how many answers the tenant may have in progress together, on all of its servers, 1 to 50 and 5 by default. A further message is refused with `429` and a `Retry-After` until one ends.
- **Steps in one answer** (`assistant_max_steps_per_turn`): the most model and tool calls one answer may take before it stops, 1 to 100 and 20 by default. A kind of answer with a lower step limit of its own keeps it; free chat's is 8.

A change leaves an `assistant.config` audit event, under the **Admin change** family, whose detail gives each setting that changed as its value before and after. A save that changes nothing leaves none. The assistant holds no key of its own, so no setting here can carry one: the model keys are read from the server's environment and never reach the console.

## Things that cannot be undone, or that end somebody's work

- Turning **Enabled** off under **General** disables the whole tenant. You type the tenant's name to confirm, every session ends and nobody can sign in until you turn it on again.
- **Clear all lockouts** cannot be undone. It forgets failures for accounts that really were being attacked as well as for the ones that were not.
- Shortening the retention period means older events are removed on the next retention pass. A removed event is gone.
- Lowering a lifetime or a limit applies to new tokens and sessions. A person who already holds a longer one keeps it until it ends, unless you also end their sessions.

## Do the same through the API

The calls are in the [settings area of the admin API](../reference/generated/admin-api/settings/index.md), with the lockout call in [lockouts](../reference/generated/admin-api/lockouts/index.md) and the assistant's settings in the [assistant area](../reference/generated/admin-api/assistant/index.md). The settings of the whole deployment, which no tenant changes, are in the [configuration reference](../reference/generated/configuration.md). From the command line, `odudu seed tenant --set` changes one for a first installation.
